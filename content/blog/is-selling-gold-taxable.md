It's one of the most common questions we hear, and a fair one to ask before you sell: does selling gold or silver create a tax obligation? Here's what's generally true, and why the specific answer depends on your own situation more than you might expect.

## The general rule

In the United States, precious metals are generally treated as a capital asset by the IRS. That means if you sell gold, silver, or coins for more than what you originally paid (or more than their value when you inherited them), that gain can potentially be subject to capital gains tax — similar to selling a stock or other investment.

## Why "it depends" is the honest answer

Several factors affect whether — and how much — tax applies to a specific sale:

**How you acquired it.** Gold you purchased yourself is treated differently than gold you inherited. Inherited items generally get a "stepped-up" cost basis (their value at the time you inherited them, not what the original owner paid), which can significantly change any calculation.

**How long you've held it.** How long you've owned an item before selling can affect which tax rate applies.

**Your overall financial picture.** Tax outcomes depend on your full financial situation, not just the sale itself.

Because every one of these varies person to person, there's no single answer that applies to everyone equally — which is exactly why we're not able to tell you what you'll owe, and why we wouldn't want to guess.

## What we can and can't help with

We can give you a clear, transparent record of your sale — the date, the items, the weight and purity, and the price paid — since that documentation is exactly what you'd want on hand if you do need to report anything. What we can't do is tell you your specific tax obligation, because that requires knowing your full financial picture, which is squarely a tax professional's job, not a precious metals buyer's.

## Our honest recommendation

If you're selling a small amount of jewelry you've simply decided you no longer want, this is often a non-issue in practice. If you're selling a larger collection, an inherited estate, or anything with meaningful value, it's worth a quick conversation with a CPA or tax preparer — before or after the sale — so you know exactly where you stand.
